## What the method assesses

The Shoulder Tool estimates a daily dose of cumulative damage (CD) to each shoulder from repeated loading, and the probability of a shoulder outcome from that dose. The source describes it in these terms:

- **Purpose:** "The tool estimates cumulative damage (CD) based on shoulder moments and loading cycles using an S–N curve derived from in vitro tendon fatigue failure tests. If multiple shoulder tasks are performed, the CD for each is summed." (abstract, PDF p. 2 (journal p. 39)). Section 4 describes a tool that "provides a CD measure for the shoulder representing cumulative loading on the shoulders during a workday" (PDF p. 9 (journal p. 46)).
- **Tissue basis:** "Tendons of the shoulder are musculoskeletal tissues of particular concern" (PDF p. 4 (journal p. 41)). The damage model uses an S–N curve from tests of "ninety specimens of the human extensor digitorum longus (EDL) tendon" (PDF p. 4 (journal p. 41)).
- **Outcome:** "The Shoulder Tool provides the probability of a clinic visit for a shoulder MSD associated with a particular job, based on the relationship shown above." (PDF p. 9 (journal p. 46)). The paper fits five outcomes and doesn't say which one the tool's probability uses (OQ-201).
- **Body region:** Upper limb. The source's term is "shoulder." The controlled vocabulary maps it to the region "upper limb."
- **Body side:** Right and left, each analyzed on its own: "The tool provides analysis for each shoulder separately, thus the greatest lever arm for one shoulder does not need to happen at the same time as the other one." (PDF p. 9 (journal p. 46)). How the two shoulders combine into a result is open (OQ-202).
- **Job types:** Monotask jobs (section 4.1), "jobs with multiple shoulder tasks" (section 4.2), and "highly variable shoulder jobs" through a binning procedure (section 4.3) (PDF pp. 9-13 (journal pp. 46-50)).
- **Intended use:** "The Shoulder Tool can be used for both comparative redesign purposes and as a screening tool." (PDF p. 14 (journal p. 51)).

### Development and validation basis

The paper develops and validates the tool on one epidemiology database. These facts bound how a result can be read:

- **Database:** "The epidemiology study was executed in six different plant sites of a large US automotive manufacturer over the period of February 1998 to September 1999 (Sesek 1999). A total of 1022 subjects participated with male subjects representing 72.7% of the workforce." Ages were "20–70 years (41.0 ± 10.9 years SD)," heights ranged "from 147 to 203 cm (174.8 ± 9.4 cm SD)," and weights were "between 45 and 159 kg (84.8 ± 17.7 kg SD)" (PDF p. 4 (journal p. 41)). Workers were included if they "had been on a cyclic job for more than 30 days and agreed to be filmed" (PDF p. 4 (journal p. 41)).
- **Jobs and tasks:** "Two hundred sixteen randomly selected jobs were included in the analysis, which consisted of a total of 446 tasks. Jobs analysed were generally multitask in nature, with up to six tasks per job." (PDF p. 4 (journal p. 41)).
- **Exposure assessment:** "Video analysis was used to obtain postural estimates and the number of repetitions by measuring the duration between the start of a cycle of a job to the start of the subsequent cycle." "The most awkward shoulder posture for a task was used for postural analysis where the neutral posture was defined as arms hanging straight down by the side of the torso." (PDF p. 4 (journal p. 41)). Shoulder moments came from simulations in "3DSSPP version 6.0.6": "Both the postural estimates and the loads carried were used for moment calculations." (PDF p. 5 (journal p. 42)). The development moments therefore didn't come from a measured lever arm and load, and the paper doesn't compare the two.
- **Validation sample:** "In the validation, 293 workers were evaluated for five separate shoulder outcomes." (PDF p. 2 (journal p. 39)). The outcomes are pain today, pain last year, the job-level first time office visit (FTOV) record, and two contrasts of job attribution, "1,2 vs. 4,5" and "1 vs. 5" (PDF p. 7 (journal p. 44); Table 3, PDF p. 9 (journal p. 46)). "First Time Office Visits (FTOVs) for shoulder/neck symptoms were also reported." (PDF p. 4 (journal p. 41)). "To be considered a case, the VAS score had to exceed 15 mm" (PDF p. 7 (journal p. 44)).
- **Covariates:** "Sex, age, plant site and body mass index (BMI) were included as covariates for the adjusted binary logistic regression model." For FTOV, "only plant site was included in the analysis as a covariate." (PDF p. 7 (journal p. 44)).
- **Exposure and outcome on different workers:** "It is important to note that when multiple workers completed the same job, the subject reporting the injury was not necessarily the same subject observed during exposure assessment." (PDF p. 4 (journal p. 41)).

Two printed inconsistencies in the validation results don't affect the calculation. They're recorded for the reviewer:

- The abstract says the odds ratios "ranged from 2.12 to 5.20" (PDF p. 2 (journal p. 39)), and the conclusion says "ranged from 2.12 to 5.19" (PDF p. 15 (journal p. 52)). Table 3 prints 5.20 (PDF p. 9 (journal p. 46)).
- Table 2 gives 47 cases for pain today (PDF p. 8 (journal p. 45)), and Table 3 gives 46 (PDF p. 9 (journal p. 46)).

## Applicability and scope checks

The paper names three loading conditions that the tool doesn't account for. Part 2 of the build prompt says "When a task falls outside a method's stated scope, say so and don't calculate," and it uses the Shoulder Tool's arm above the shoulder as its example. This specification therefore proposes that the assessor answers each condition yes or no for every task, and that the engine doesn't calculate a task with a yes answer:

| Condition recorded by the assessor                                                                            | Source wording (PDF p. 15 (journal p. 52))                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The arm is held straight in line with the shoulder, so that the force passes through it, as in a forward push | "it does not account for all loading conditions such as when an arm is held straight in line with the shoulder such as a push forward action when stress in the shoulder will be generated even though no moment is present"             |
| The arm is held straight above the shoulder                                                                   | "Another example is when the arm is held straight above the shoulder. Although a low moment is created, intensive muscle contraction is taking place and this loading condition can contribute to physiological (and material) fatigue." |
| The task involves continuous overhead work                                                                    | "Also, the tool does not account for the additional load associated with continuous overhead work."                                                                                                                                      |

These points support the checks:

- Eq 2 defines the total as "the daily dose for the CD for all tasks" (PDF p. 4 (journal p. 41)). A total that leaves out an out-of-scope task would understate that dose, so the proposal also withholds the job's total CD and probability for that shoulder, and names the task that stopped the calculation. The reviewer confirms or changes this proposal under OQ-203.
- "To estimate the CD, data collection is needed to obtain a complete job description" (PDF p. 15 (journal p. 52)). The total covers only the tasks the assessor enters.
- The paper lists "type of task being performed" as an input (PDF p. 9 (journal p. 46)) but gives no list of task types and no rule for how a type changes the calculation (OQ-203). See "Lever arm by task type."

- Table 4 note: "Note: Moment includes a moment due to the weight of the forearm and hand based on lever arm." (PDF p. 13 (journal p. 50)).
- Figure 11 caption: "Note that The Shoulder Tool adds a moment due to the forearm and hand weight based on lever arm." (PDF p. 14 (journal p. 51)).

The paper prints no formula, segment weight, or center-of-mass location for that moment. Both transcription passes searched the whole paper and recorded "not printed" (`text-constants.reconciled.csv`, key `forearm-moment:formula`). The engine can't compute a task moment from this source (OQ-200).

### Stress as a percentage of ultimate strength

The paper describes the step from moment to stress in words only: "To obtain the number of cycles to failure, the ultimate strength of the shoulders needs to be estimated. Both the shoulder moments at each shoulder joint and the shoulder strength capability were used to estimate the ultimate strength for the shoulder. The shoulder strength capability estimates were obtained from 3DSSPP (50% male population, lifting standing, average for left and right shoulders)." (PDF pp. 5-6 (journal pp. 42-43)). Figure 1 places a box reading "% Ultimate strength 50th %ile male" between the 3DSSPP "Shoulder strength" and "Shoulder moment" boxes and the "Cycles to failure" box (PDF p. 5 (journal p. 42)).

The paper prints no strength or UTS value and no formula that turns a moment into S. Both passes recorded "not printed" (key `strength:numeric`). The paper names 3DSSPP as the origin of the strength estimates but doesn't give the value, a posture beyond "lifting standing," or how strength and moment combine, so a 3DSSPP run by this build couldn't be tied to the tool's value (OQ-200).

### Cycles to failure (Eq 1)

The paper gives the S–N relationship as Eq 1:

```text
S = 101.25 − 14.83 log(N)        (Eq 1; PDF p. 4 (journal p. 41); Figure 1, PDF p. 5 (journal p. 42))
```

"where S is the normalised stress as a percentage of the ultimate tensile strength (UTS), and N is number of cycles to failure (Schechtman and Bader 1997)." The equation "describes the exponential relationship between the applied stress and median fatigue life (50% probability of failure) for the EDL from which the damage per cycle (DPC) can be estimated at a certain stress level." The data came from "ninety specimens of the human extensor digitorum longus (EDL) tendon" that "were tested using 10 specimens at nine different stress levels." (PDF p. 4 (journal p. 41)). These details apply to Eq 1:

- **Constants:** 101.25 and 14.83, from `text-constants.reconciled.csv` (keys `eq1:text` and `eq1:figure-1`). Figure 1 prints the minus sign as a hyphen ("S = 101.25 - 14.83 log(N)"). The transcription stores it as U+2212 under D-116.
- **Rearranged, not printed:** log(N) = (101.25 − S) / 14.83. N is the antilogarithm of that quotient in the base that the paper doesn't state (OQ-206).
- **Domain:** The paper states none. At S = 101.25, log(N) = 0 and N = 1 whatever the base (recomputation). The paper doesn't say what applies at or past that point (OQ-205).

### Damage per cycle

The paper defines damage per cycle in words: "DPC for various levels of stress is an important factor for the shoulder tool development and is derived by dividing 1 by the number of cycles to failure at a particular level of stress." (PDF p. 4 (journal p. 41)). "Equation (1) was then used to get the number of cycles to failure for left and right shoulders from which the reciprocal was equal to the DPC." (PDF p. 6 (journal p. 43)).

```text
DPC = 1 / N        (stated in words; PDF p. 4 (journal p. 41); PDF p. 6 (journal p. 43))
```

### Cumulative damage (Eq 2)

The paper sums the damage of all tasks into a daily dose:

```text
Total CD = Σ (i = 1 to j) DPC_i × n_i        (Eq 2; PDF p. 4 (journal p. 41); Figure 1, PDF p. 5 (journal p. 42))
```

"where Total CD is the daily dose for the CD for all tasks, DPCi is the damage per cycle for task i, i is number of tasks in a job with a total of j tasks, ni is the number of repetitions within task i (Gallagher et al. 2018)." (PDF p. 4 (journal p. 41)). Figure 1 prints the lower limit of the sum as "i," without "=1" (`text-constants.reconciled.csv`, key `eq2:figure-1`). These points apply to Eq 2:

- **Each shoulder:** "Equation (2) was then used to calculate the job CD for the left and right shoulder separately. The job CD was estimated by summing the multiplication of the DPC for each task by the corresponding number of repetitions completed per workday." (PDF p. 6 (journal p. 43)).
- **Task CD:** The product `DPC_i × n_i` is the task's term of the sum, shown for each task as "Damage (cumulative)" in Figures 8 to 11. Section 4.2 says the tool "provides a daily dose CD for each shoulder by summing the CD for each task" (PDF p. 11 (journal p. 48)).
- **Order:** The engine sums in task-row order. This is part 2 of the build prompt's fixed-order rule, not a source rule.
- **Table 1:** The worked table multiplies DPC by repetitions per minute and reports "CD/day" without printing the minute-to-day multiplier (OQ-204). See "Worked examples for the published-example tests."

### Percentage of total damage

The paper describes the share in words: "Moreover, the tool provides the percentage contribution for the total CD associated with each task." (PDF p. 11 (journal p. 48)). It prints no formula. This specification reads the wording as 100 × task CD ÷ total CD for the same shoulder. Recomputed from the printed damages in Figures 10 and 11, that reading gives every printed percentage to one decimal place.

The source uses the share to set priority within a job: "In this example, task 3 is associated with the highest percentage of CD, thus a priority should be given to task 3 in terms of ergonomic intervention strategies." (PDF p. 11 (journal p. 48)).

### Larger-shoulder CD

The validation used the larger of the two shoulders' job CD, and the paper states it twice:

- "An assumption was also made that the higher CD value between the shoulders (left or right) would be responsible for a higher probability of a shoulder outcome." (PDF p. 6 (journal p. 43)).
- "The highest value of CD for the left and right shoulders was used in the analysis under the assumption that the highest value is associated with a higher probability of a shoulder MSD." (PDF p. 7 (journal p. 44)).

Table 1 shows the result as "CD MAX":

```text
CD MAX = max(JOB CD RS, JOB CD LS)        (PDF p. 6 (journal p. 43); PDF p. 7 (journal p. 44); Table 1)
```

The paper states this rule for the validation. It doesn't say whether the calculator's probability uses the larger shoulder or each shoulder's own total (OQ-202).

### Probability of an outcome (Eqs 3 and 4)

"Regression equations were used to determine the relationship between the log of CD measure and the probability of an outcome (positive case) using the following equation:" (PDF p. 7 (journal p. 44)):

```text
P(event) = exp(Y′)/(1 + exp(Y′))        (Eq 3; PDF p. 7 (journal p. 44))
Y′ = β0 + β1 × Log CD                   (Eq 4; PDF p. 7 (journal p. 44))
```

"where Y′ relationship will be derived from the logistic regression equation" and "Thus, this regression equation can be used to estimate the probability of a shoulder outcome for each value of CD." (PDF p. 7 (journal p. 44)).

The paper doesn't print these parts of the calculation:

- **Coefficients:** No numeric β0 or β1 for any outcome. Both passes recorded "not printed" (keys `eq4:beta0-numeric` and `eq4:beta1-numeric`). Table 3 gives odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals for Log CD and the covariates (PDF p. 9 (journal p. 46)), and Figure 3 plots the five fitted curves without their equations (PDF p. 10 (journal p. 47)). The paper doesn't say to derive coefficients from either, and under operating rule 1 neither is a source for β0 or β1 (OQ-200).
- **Model:** Which of the five outcomes the tool uses, and whether the model is crude or adjusted (OQ-201).
- **Base:** The base of "Log" (OQ-206).
- **Shoulder:** Which total CD the probability takes when the shoulders differ (OQ-202).

#### Observation from the screenshots

This subsection is the build's recomputation. It isn't a source, and this specification doesn't use it. Figures 8 to 11 each show a total CD and a probability:

| --------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Figure 8  | 0.00441                 | 21.0                                |
| Figure 9  | 0.03495                 | 38.0                                |
| Figure 10 | 0.04217                 | 39.8                                |
| Figure 11 | 0.14564                 | 52.2                                |

Recomputed by the build, the logit of each probability, ln(P/(1 − P)), against log10 of the total CD gives pairwise slopes from 0.929 to 0.932 for the six pairs. The four pairs are therefore consistent with one logistic line in log10 CD. This observation isn't a source for β0 or β1. Operating rule 1 and the source-fidelity rules in part 2 of the build prompt forbid inferring a coefficient, and using this observation would need the product owner's explicit decision. This specification recommends against it, for these reasons:

- The four points are screenshot values, rounded to one decimal place for the probability and five decimal places for the CD, from a calculator version the paper doesn't name.
- The fit can't identify the outcome model (OQ-201) or the log base (OQ-206). A logit that is linear in log10 CD is also linear in ln CD.
- The fit gives no damage per cycle, which also needs the strength value and the forearm and hand moment (OQ-200).

### Binning procedure

For highly variable jobs, the paper groups tasks into bins: "For jobs with highly variable shoulder intensive tasks, a binning procedure can be used." (PDF p. 11 (journal p. 48)). The procedure has three statements:

- "For this analysis, tasks can be grouped into bins based on their peak moments and the repetitions for the tasks in the same bin can be summed to arrive at the repetitions for each bin. In this example, three bins will be used for the analysis, bin 1 (1–15 Nm), bin 2 (16–30 Nm), and bin 3 (31–45 Nm). The tasks will be then grouped into their corresponding bin and the repetitions will be summed for the tasks within each bin." (PDF p. 11 (journal p. 48)).
- Each bin becomes one task row: "This bin will represent the first task in the shoulder tool with 157 repetitions." (PDF p. 13 (journal p. 50)).
- "some inflation in the probability may result. Thus, using the narrowest range possible for binning is recommended." (PDF p. 13 (journal p. 50)).

The example leaves these points unresolved, and each one is part of OQ-207:

- **Gaps:** The bins leave moments less than 1 N·m, between 15 and 16 N·m, and between 30 and 31 N·m in no bin, while Table 4 prints moments to one decimal place.
- **Representative moment:** Figure 11 enters the three bins with a 31 cm lever arm and loads of 30, 78.3, and 126.8 N, which the calculator turns into moments of 15.0, 30.0, and 45.0 N·m, the upper limit of each bin (PDF p. 14 (journal p. 51)). The text doesn't state that rule or how to choose a bin's lever arm and load. Because the calculator adds the forearm and hand moment, the load that gives a target moment depends on a term the paper doesn't print.
- **Truncated data:** Table 4 ends in a row of ellipses. Its nine visible items fall in the bins that the text names (items 5, 7, and 9 in bin 1; items 1, 4, 6, and 8 in bin 2; items 2 and 3 in bin 3), and their repetitions sum to 69, 60, and 61 (recomputation), against the printed totals of 157, 122, and 105, which include tasks "among others." The example can't be rebuilt from the paper.
- **Public page:** The calculator's instruction page reaches the same three totals with different tasks and different bins. See "Reference calculator" and OQ-141.
