## What the method assesses

The method estimates the maximum load or force that a stated share of female or male workers would select as acceptable for a manual materials handling task over an 8-hour workday. The method is psychophysical: its outputs are loads and forces that participants selected as acceptable, not probabilities of injury.

- **Maximum acceptable load (MAL):** "The equations output the maximum acceptable load (MAL) outputs for the 50th percentile (ie. 50% capable) in kg" (section 3, PDF p. 9 (journal p. 962)).
- **Psychophysical basis:** "Participants selected weights for lift, lower, and carry tasks, or initial forces (required to get the load moving) and sustained forces (required to keep the load moving) for push and pull tasks, that would be acceptable over an 8-hour workday" (section 1, PDF pp. 2-3 (journal pp. 955-956)). The pooled values "were assumed to represent the 50th percentile MAL values for females or males" (section 2.1, PDF p. 3 (journal p. 956)).
- **Equations:** "A total of 14 equations were developed – one for each table in Snook and Ciriello (1991) – including female and male versions of (1) Lift, (2) Lower, (3) Push – Initial, (4) Push – Sustained, (5) Pull – Initial, (6) Pull – Sustained, and (7) Carry equations" (section 2.3, PDF p. 5 (journal p. 958)).
- **Variability:** "Coefficients of variation (CV) are provided so that standard deviations can be estimated with each unique MAL output from an equation" (section 3, PDF p. 9 (journal p. 962)). The CVs "facilitate: (1) the determination of maximum acceptable loads for any percentile of the working population and/or (2) the calculation of the percent capable of any given load" (section 4.2, PDF p. 14 (journal p. 967)).
- **Reference anthropometry:** "Reference heights in the present synthesis were estimated using 50th percentile values from ANSUR II (anthropometric survey of U.S. Army personnel) to be representative of the population (Gordon et al. 2014) (Table 3)" (section 2.2, PDF p. 3 (journal p. 956)).
- **Body region:** The source names no single region. It says participants "chose MALs based on the most stressed joint (Banks and Caldwell 2019) such that the MALs from our equations likely output loads acceptable to the whole body" (section 4.2, PDF p. 14 (journal p. 967)). The controlled vocabulary has no whole-body term (OQ-160).
- **Body side:** Not applicable. The lift and lower equations apply to "two-handed tasks" (section 4.3, PDF p. 15 (journal p. 968)). The source states no hand count for push, pull, or carry.
- **Sex:** Each task has a female and a male equation. The source's design criterion is stated for females: "Designing for at least 75% of females has been identified as a criterion sensitive to injury risk for both males and females" (section 3.4, PDF p. 11 (journal p. 964)). Which sex's result sets the band, and how male results are reported, is open (OQ-161).

## Applicability and scope checks

The engine selects one of the 14 equations from the task, the variant, and the sex (see "Which equation applies"). Every input must fall inside the printed constraints for the selected sex (see "Input constraints").

### Initial and sustained forces

The push and pull equations come in two variants. Initial forces are those "required to get the load moving," and sustained forces are those "required to keep the load moving" (section 1, PDF pp. 2-3 (journal pp. 955-956)). The source doesn't say whether an assessment of a push or pull task needs both variants (OQ-162).

The engine applies the constraints this way:

- **No correction:** The equations have no substitution rules. An input outside a constraint is rejected with a message that states the range for the selected sex, as part 2 of the build prompt requires ("No silent correction").
- **Arm reach:** Whether arm reach is an input or fixed at the Table 3 values, and what limits apply to the lowest hand height and to VRM, is open (OQ-168).
- **Frequency and end points:** The lower frequency bound (1/480 or 0.0021 per minute), and whether every printed bound is inclusive, are open (OQ-170).
- **Carry – Female V:** The printed range 0.71 to 1.03 m is wider than the heights the carry V_SF formulas were developed for, 0.73 and 1.02 m (OQ-171).

### Ranges the formulas were developed for

The source extended some formulas past the studied conditions. These statements explain the constraints. They add no rule.

- H: "the horizontal reach scale factor formulas (HSF) were extrapolated back to allow for minimum H values of 0.20 m and 0.25 m for females and males, respectively" (section 2.3.1, PDF p. 7 (journal p. 960)). The smallest studied reaches were "0.38 m and 0.43 m associated with the smallest box widths studied with females and males, respectively" (section 4.1, PDF p. 14 (journal p. 967)).
- DV: "In the original Liberty Mutual studies, the largest vertical displacement was DV = 0.76 m." Data from NIOSH (1981) and Mital, Nicholson, and Ayoub (1993) "were incorporated to determine DVSF formulas for inputs up to DV = 1.96 m for females and DV = 2.14 m for males" (section 2.3.1, PDF pp. 6-7 (journal pp. 959-960)).
- Velocity: the highest mean velocities studied were "(0.51 m)(20/min) = 10.2 m/min" for lifting and lowering (section 2.3.1, PDF p. 6 (journal p. 959)), "(15.2 m)(2.4/min) = 36.5 m/min" for pushing or pulling (section 2.3.2, PDF p. 8 (journal p. 961)), and "(8.5 m)(3.3/min) = 28.1 m/min" for carrying (section 2.3.3, PDF p. 9 (journal p. 962)).

## Equations

### General form

The source prints one form for each task group. Square brackets written side by side multiply, as the worked example shows ("MAL = 34.9 [0.766] [0.948] [0.907] [0.677] = 15.56 kg," PDF p. 11 (journal p. 964)).

```text
Lift and Lower:   MAL = RL [H_SF] [VRM_SF] [DV_SF] [F_SF]     (section 3.1, PDF p. 9 (journal p. 962))
Push and Pull:    MAL = RL [V_SF] [DH_SF] [F_SF]              (section 3.2, PDF p. 10 (journal p. 963))
Carry:            MAL = RL [V_SF] [DH_SF] [F_SF]              (section 3.3, PDF p. 10 (journal p. 963))
```

"RL is the maximum possible (reference) load from the equations (kg)" (where-clauses, PDF pp. 9-10 (journal pp. 962-963)). "ln" is the natural logarithm: section 2.3 gives "ln[2.1] = 0.742, ln[4.3] = 1.459 and ln[8.5] = 2.140" (PDF p. 6 (journal p. 959)).

### Derived quantities

The source defines these quantities from the inputs.

```text
DV  = |V_ORIGIN − V_DEST|                    (Figure 1, PDF p. 7 (journal p. 960))
VRM = (V_ORIGIN + V_DEST)/2                  (Figure 1, PDF p. 7 (journal p. 960))
Maximum vertical height of the hand = VRM + DV/2           (PDF p. 9 (journal p. 962))
Mean vertical velocity   = DV x F   (lift, lower)          (PDF p. 10 (journal p. 963))
Mean horizontal velocity = DH x F   (push, pull, carry)    (PDF p. 10 (journal p. 963); PDF p. 11 (journal p. 964))
```

### Meaning of ln(F)²

Every squared frequency term is printed as ln(F) with the superscript 2 after the closing parenthesis, and the worked example writes "ln(1)²" (PDF p. 11 (journal p. 964)). Both transcription passes read it that way. The term can mean [ln(F)]² or ln(F²). The worked example uses F = 1, where both readings give zero. Section 2.3 describes "1st, 2nd and 3rd order polynomial ‘scale factor formulas’" fitted to "the natural log of each level of the independent variables" (PDF pp. 5-6 (journal pp. 958-959)). The engine doesn't fix the reading until OQ-172 is answered.

### Scale factors greater than 1.0

Section 2.3 says each scale factor formula "was subsequently normalised to that maximum value so that the resulting formula never predicted a value above 1.0 for the range of inputs," and that "the reference load for each equation represented the highest possible MAL magnitude from that equation" (PDF p. 6 (journal p. 959)). The printed equations have no cap. Recomputed for this specification, with the [ln(F)]² reading, at the printed input bounds:

- F_SF reaches 1.00050 at F = 1/480 for Push – Sustained – Male and Pull – Sustained – Male, and 1.00046 for Carry – Female (recomputed by the build).
- The Carry – Female V_SF reaches 1.00448 at V = 0.71 m (OQ-171).
- Every other scale factor peaks between 0.99996 and 1.00005 inside the printed ranges (for VRM, from 0 to arm reach).

Whether the engine caps scale factors at 1.0 is open (OQ-173).

### Coupling scale factor

The source recommends a coupling scale factor for lift and lower in its limitations section, not in the equations: "So, for lift and lower conditions with no handles, we recommend an additional coupling scale factor (CSF) 0.84. ## Limitations to show on every result

These statements come from the source and appear with every LM-MMH result:

4. "As with the revised Liberty Mutual Tables, the proposed Lift, Lower and Carry equations do not account for box length or height" (section 4.3, PDF p. 15 (journal p. 968)).

6. "the outputs from the LM-MMH Equations should be used with caution when many tasks are performed in sequence. Energy expenditure limits (Garg, Chaffin, and Herrin 1978; Dempsey et al. 2008) and other methods based on cumulative low back loading (Gallagher et al. 2017; Marras et al. 2014) should be considered when tasks are combined" (section 4.3, PDF p. 15 (journal p. 968)).

8. 9. "While our equations are based on all the data from 40 years of research, it is possible that the inclusion of the older data does not fully reflect potential secular decreases in tolerance" (section 4.1, PDF p. 14 (journal p. 967)).
10. "While these equations can inform acceptable physical loads for occupational task designs that control musculoskeletal pain, injury reporting and length of disability, practitioners should be aware that personal, work environment and psychosocial factors can also have a significant effect on these outcomes (Shaw et al. 2006)" (section 4.3, PDF p. 15 (journal p. 968)).
