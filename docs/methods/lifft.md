### Findings in the calculator workbook

The build found these defects and inconsistencies in the workbook. The specification's equations follow the workbook as stored. Where a finding needs a decision, an open question records it:

1. **Rounding inside the calculation:** Each task's damage is rounded to four decimal places before the total is summed (L5 and L32, lines 10 and 12; N34, line 14), and the probability comes from that rounded total (N35, line 16). A task whose damage is less than 0.00005 contributes nothing (OQ-189).
2. **A probability of 0 for a total of 0:** N35 returns 0 when the total is 0, through "IF($N$34=0,0,...)" (line 16). The damage-to-risk relationship never produces 0, and the logarithm of 0 is undefined (OQ-233).
3. **Two damage relationships:** The hidden moment_cd_relationship sheet holds a table of damage per cycle and 2,000 interpolation formulas that no formula on the main sheet uses (manifest note). The formulas select no row, and their cached values are errors, "#VALUE!" (single reading). The table differs from the main sheet's closed form by between 2.8% less (at 96 ft·lb) and 6.7% more (at 3 ft·lb), recomputed by the build (OQ-231).
4. **Instruction screenshots that the workbook's formulas don't reproduce:** The multi-task and binning screenshots (Instructions sheet, Figures 3 and 4) show task damages, totals, shares, and probabilities that the main sheet's formulas don't give for the same inputs. Interpolating in the hidden sheet's table reproduces them (OQ-231).
5. **Instruction text that rounds down:** The text reports "a 40% probability" for the mono-task example (instruction page, "Assessing Mono-Task Jobs"; Instructions!B25, single reading), where the screenshot shows 40.8 and the workbook gives 0.408. It reports "51%" where the multi-task screenshot shows 51.4 (instruction page, "Assessing Jobs with Multiple Lifting Tasks"). Both are consistent with truncation, not rounding (OQ-186).
6. **A stray formula:** Cell O5 holds `IF(OR(E5="",G5="",K5=""),"",IF($N$34=0,0,ROUND(M5/$N$34, 5)*100))`, which refers to spacer columns that hold no input, and no cell refers to O5 (single reading). It looks like the share formula shifted by one column, with five decimal places instead of four.
7. **Input checks on the wrong cells:** Only task 1's load has an effective check: a decimal of at least 0 on F5. The other validation rules sit on cells that hold no input (F6:F7, F9:F10, and so on to F30:F31, and G5:G32), and a list rule on D6 refers to the defined name "Scale" (single reading; OQ-236).
8. **Leftovers from the DUET workbook:** The defined names "Scale," "ScaleLookup," "RiskValue," and "EmptyCell" refer to an external workbook named `DUET_v1.3.xlsm`, and the external-link part lists sheet names from both tools, among them "total_damage_risk_relationship," a sheet that isn't in this file (single reading).
9. **A wrong link:** Cell B39 reads "Lastest Version: http://lifft.pythonanywhere.com/," but its hyperlink targets `http://duet.pythonanywhere.com/` (single reading).
10. **Two color scales on one cell:** N35 carries two three-color scales with the same points, 0.05, 0.3, and 0.6, and different middle colors (single reading). Neither matches the paper's 25% and 50% bands (OQ-187).
11. **Different blank tests:** N35 tests the share cells (N5 to N32) for blanks, while N34 tests the damage cells (L5 to L32) (lines 14 and 16). Both tests give the same answer, because a share cell is blank exactly when its damage cell is. This changes no result.

## What the method assesses

LiFFT estimates the cumulative low back load of a job's lifting tasks and relates the daily total to the probability that the job is a high-risk job. The sources describe it in these terms:

- **Purpose (manuscript):** "the Lifting Fatigue Failure Tool (“LiFFT”), that can be used to estimate cumulative spinal loading associated with lifting tasks with three simple inputs (load weight, peak horizontal distance from spine to load, and repetition)" (section 1, converted PDF pp. 1-2).
- **Purpose (calculator):** "The purpose of the LiFFT tool is to determine the cumulative low back load experienced during a workday. Based on LiFFT’s cumulative damage measure, a probability that the job is high risk is calculated" (instruction page, "Using LiFFT"; Instructions!B13, single reading).
- **Goal:** "Our goal in developing this tool was to develop a user-friendly method to estimate the risk of LBP/LBDs resulting from CD associated with variable magnitude loads and lifting repetitions" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 2).
- **Summary:** The tool "requires only the PLM (horizontal distance from spine to load times the weight of load) for a lifting task and number of repetitions performed for that task. A measure of CD is calculated based upon these values and can be summed across numerous tasks to derive a “daily dose” of CD" (section 5 (Conclusions), converted PDF pp. 9-10).
- **Model chain:** "Estimates of CD were developed by estimating the spinal compression associated with each LM, comparing these compression estimates to the compressive strength of an “average” spine (approximately 6 kN; Jager and Luttmann, 1991), and multiplying the calculated damage per cycle (DPC; derived from studies of fatigue failure of spinal motion segments) by the number of repetitions experienced during the task at hand" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 2). The calculator replaces the steps from the moment to the damage per cycle with one closed-form function of the moment (see "Equations").
- **Outcome behind the probability:** The calculator defines it: "* A “High Risk Job” is defined as a job experiencing 12+ injuries per 200,000 hours worked, as defined by Marras et al. (1993)." (LiFFT_v1.4.0!A47, line 17). The manuscript's LMM database "consists of two levels for LBD outcome. Low-risk jobs were categorized as those having no injuries and no turnover for the preceding three-year period. High-risk jobs were those having at least 12 injuries per 200,000 hours of exposure" (section 3, converted PDF p. 4). Zelik et al. use "LBD Risk" to mean "the probability of a job being a high-risk job" (section 2.1).
- **Body region:** Back (low back). The distance is measured from "the L5-S1 vertebral segment" (section 2.2, converted PDF p. 3), and the outcomes are "LBP/LBDs" throughout. The controlled vocabulary maps this to the region "back."
- **Body side:** The sources don't address body side, and the method takes no side input.
- **Sex and anthropometry:** The method takes no sex or anthropometry input. The compression model used "an individual of average anthropometry (blended male and female)" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 3).

### Development and validation basis

These statements describe how the authors built and tested the method. They're context for the reviewer and for the limitations, not calculation steps:

- **Fatigue data behind Eq. (1):** Cadaveric data from Brinckmann et al. (1988) and Gallagher et al. (2005, 2007) "were analyzed using a Weibull approach to estimate the probability of spine failure at varying levels of estimated ultimate strength of motion segments" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 2). For censored specimens, the authors estimated "the number of cycles to reach a given criterion level of damage (10 mm displacement)" by "linear estimation" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 2).
- **LMM database:** 235 jobs, 124 low-risk and 111 high-risk (section 3, converted PDF p. 4; section 3.1, converted PDF p. 5). "In the LMM database, Lifting Frequency is provided in lifts/hr, so this value was multiplied by eight to get an estimate of the daily dose of CD" (section 3.1, converted PDF p. 5). The conclusions describe these as "mono-task jobs (i.e., jobs with no rotation)" (section 5 (Conclusions), converted PDF p. 10).
- **Effect of the LMM design:** "The strength of relationship for the LBD outcome from this database may be due in part to the fact that low risk and high risk outcomes were separated by eliminating the medium risk category, making the relationship more pronounced" (section 5 (Discussion), converted PDF p. 8).
- **Automotive database:** 667 jobs, with 304 workers analyzed in six subsets of 179-304 subjects (section 3, converted PDF pp. 4-5). "Only jobs with well-defined lifting activities were included (administrative jobs or jobs that did not have well defined tasks were not analyzed)" (section 3.2, converted PDF p. 5). Jobs had "several different tasks (up to six), for which CD was calculated by summing the exposure calculated for each task" (section 3.2, converted PDF p. 6).
- **Automotive subjects:** "The subjects ranged in height from 147-203 cm (mean = 174.8 cm ± 9.4), weighed between 45-159 kg (mean = 84.8 kg ± 17.7), and were 20-70 years of age (mean = 41.6 years ± 10.9). The subjects were 72.7% male and 27.3% female" (section 3, converted PDF p. 5).
- **Later updates:** The calculator's version history records "V1.3.0 - 11/06/2017: updated damage - risk relationship and color coding calculation" and "V1.4.0 - 06/05/2018: updated damage - risk relationship" (instruction page, version history). No source the build has found says what data the updates used.

### Measurement guidance

The sources give this guidance for collecting the inputs. The application shows it at the point of entry:

- "Prior to using LiFFT, a task analysis is recommended to properly define all relevant work tasks that comprise a job" (section 2.2, converted PDF p. 3).
- "In general, we recommend following a “sampling approach” that requires that task data be obtained from a subset of the lifts to determine a representative lift that occurs during a work shift" (section 2.2, converted PDF p. 3).
- "Marras, Allread, and Reid (1998) have suggested observing 7-10 repetitions as a general rule of thumb. However, it is important to note that this may not be possible for infrequent lifts (that also may happen to be the heaviest or most awkward, and have the largest peak moment). Therefore, the more frequent a job task, the more task cycles we recommend be observed to ensure that a representative lift be captured" (section 2.2, converted PDF p. 3).

### Notes on the input definitions

These points affect how the inputs are defined and recorded:

- **Reference point for the distance:** The manuscript's user instruction names "the L5-S1 vertebral segment" (section 2.2, converted PDF pp. 3-4). Other passages say "spine," for example "peak horizontal distance from spine to load" (section 1, converted PDF p. 2), and the automotive validation measured the distance "according to RNLE method" (section 3.2, converted PDF p. 6). The version of record adds the greater trochanter as an estimate of L5-S1 (p. 143), the calculator asks for the distance "from the hip joint" (instruction page, "Using LiFFT"), and Zelik et al. say "the peak horizontal distance from the lumbar spine to the load" (section 2.1). Which wording the application shows is open (OQ-235).
- **Weight, mass, and force:** The calculator takes the load in pounds and multiplies it by the lever arm in feet to give "ft.lbs." (LiFFT_v1.4.0!H3 and H5, lines 5 and 9), so it treats the pound value as a force. The manuscript calls the input "weight" but gives it in kilograms, and the automotive validation multiplied the distance by "the known load mass" (section 3.2, converted PDF p. 6). The conversion of a metric input is open (OQ-183).
- **Workday:** Repetitions are counted per workday. No source defines the length of a workday. Only the LMM validation assumed one, through its multiplier of eight (section 3.1, converted PDF p. 5).
- **Provenance:** Because the manuscript accepts "a count (or estimate)," the application records whether the repetitions were counted or estimated, as part 2 of the build prompt requires.

### Moment

```text

```

In this specification's notation:

```text
moment (ft·lb) = lever arm (in) / 12 × load (lb)
```

The heading is "Moment \n(ft.lbs.)" (LiFFT_v1.4.0!H3, line 5). The manuscript defines the peak load moment as "the weight of the lifted object multiplied by the horizontal distance of the load to the spine" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 2), and its example multiplies a mass by a length, "(40 cm × 13 kg = 51 Nm)" (section 2.2, converted PDF p. 4). ### Damage for each task

The formula as stored, identical in L32 apart from the row numbers:

```text
IF(OR(D5="", F5="", J5=""),"", ROUND(10^(-5.81636221052493+0.0222878437409416*H5)*J5, 4))        LiFFT_v1.4.0!L5 (line 10); L32 (line 12)
```

In this specification's notation:

```text
damage per lift = 10^(-5.81636221052493 + 0.0222878437409416 × moment)
task damage     = ROUND(damage per lift × repetitions, 4)
```

These points apply:

- **Constants:** The two constants, `-5.81636221052493` and `0.0222878437409416`, are stored in the formula. The moment enters in ft·lb, and `10^` raises 10 to the power.
- **Names:** The workbook computes the damage per lift inside L5 and doesn't display it. The manuscript calls that quantity the "damage per cycle" (DPC). The cell's heading is "Damage\n(cumulative)" (LiFFT_v1.4.0!L3, line 7), and this specification calls the cell's value the task damage.
- **Rounding:** `ROUND(..., 4)` rounds the task damage to four decimal places before the total is summed. Part 2 of the build prompt requires full precision until display, so whether the engine reproduces this step is open (OQ-189).
- **The hidden sheet:** The workbook also holds a tabulated damage relationship that no formula uses, and the instruction screenshots follow that table instead of this formula (OQ-231; see "The hidden moment_cd_relationship sheet").

The formula as stored:


In this specification's notation:

```text
total = task damage(1) + task damage(2) + ... + task damage(10), skipping blank tasks
```

The label is "Total Cumulative Damage:" (LiFFT_v1.4.0!H34, line 13). The total is the sum of the rounded task damages. ### Probability of a high-risk job

The formula as stored:


These points apply:

- **Scale:** The probability is a fraction from 0 to 1, rounded to three decimal places. The cell's number format shows it as a percentage with one decimal place, "0.0%" (single reading), so 0.408 appears as 40.8%.
- **Label and outcome:** The label is "Probability of High Risk Job * (%):" (LiFFT_v1.4.0!H35, line 15), and the asterisk refers to the definition in A47 (line 17), quoted under "What the method assesses."

### The manuscript's model chain and Eq. (1)

The manuscript describes the steps from the moment to the damage per cycle without printing them: "We then estimated the compressive load associated with specific PLMs using a static biomechanical model (Bloswick and Villnave, 2000). Analyses were performed in an upright posture using an individual of average anthropometry (blended male and female), and varying PLMs were analyzed to develop an estimate of the compressive loads associated with various PLMs. Regression techniques were used to develop an equation defining the relationship. Using data from Jager and Luttmann (1991), we calculated the US of an “average” spine for the working age population (approximately 6 kN), again using blended males and female data for specimens aged 20-60. This allowed us to relate the “average” compressive load for a specified PLM with respect to an “average” spine, which could then be related to the percentage of US to allow estimation of the DPC at a specified level of LM" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 3).

The one equation it prints is Eq. (1), shown here with the superscript written as a caret and parentheses, from the reconciled transcription:

"where %US is the percentage of the ultimate strength for a motion segment" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 3). The damage per cycle is "the inverse" of the cycles to failure (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 3). The reconciled transcription gives these values (`text-constants.reconciled.csv`):

Every LiFFT result is decision support for a qualified person. It estimates the cumulative load that a job's lifting places on a model of an average spine and relates it to the probability that the job belongs to a group of jobs with high injury rates. It isn't a diagnosis, a finding about an individual worker, or a statement of compliance.

## Units

The calculator works in inches, pounds, and ft·lb, and its damage constants apply to a moment in ft·lb (LiFFT_v1.4.0!D3, F3, H3, and H5, lines 3, 4, 5, and 9). The manuscript works in kilograms, centimeters, newton-meters (printed "Nm"), and kilonewtons, and prints no value of g. The version 1.1 screenshots take centimeters and kilograms and show moments in N·m, and their six printed moments reproduce only with g = 9.8 m/s² (recomputed by the build; see "Corroboration and disagreements"). The web calculator's version 1.4.1 "added function for metric input" (instruction page, version history); the build hasn't seen how it converts.

The engine stores inputs in canonical SI units and computes in the calculator's units, converting with the shared factors, as part 2 of the build prompt requires ("Compute each method in the units its source uses"). Inputs in inches and pounds then enter the calculator's formulas unchanged. How a metric input converts is open (OQ-183).

The build's recomputations in this specification use the exact definitions 1 in = 0.0254 m, 1 lb = 0.45359237 kg, and standard gravity = 9.80665 m/s², so 1 ft·lb (force) = 1.3558179483314 N·m, except where a recomputation states g = 9.8. ## Rounding

The calculator rounds at three points inside its calculation, all transcribed:

- **Task damage:** to four decimal places, before the total is summed (LiFFT_v1.4.0!L5 and L32, lines 10 and 12).
- **Share:** the fraction to four decimal places, then multiplied by 100 (LiFFT_v1.4.0!N5, line 11).
- **Probability:** the fraction to three decimal places (LiFFT_v1.4.0!N35, line 16).

The moment and the total aren't rounded in the calculation. The calculator displays the moment, the share, and the probability to one decimal place, and the damages and the total to four (single reading; see "Values read once by the build"). No source states how a value that ends exactly in 5 is rounded; if OQ-189 adopts the calculator's rounding steps, that mode needs a decision too.

The manuscript states no rounding rule. It prints values at these precisions, all in section 2.2 (converted PDF p. 4):

- Peak load moment: a whole number of newton-meters ("51 Nm").
- Damage per cycle: "0.000011," two significant figures.
- Daily cumulative damage: "0.0043," two significant figures.
- Risk: "25.39%" in the mono-task example, but a whole percentage ("35%") in the multi-task example.
- Percentage of cumulative load: a whole percentage ("56%") in the multi-task example.

- **Calculation:** Full precision, as part 2 of the build prompt requires, unless the product owner adopts the calculator's rounding steps (OQ-189). Recomputed by the build, the choice changes the calculator's mono-task example from 40.8% (with the rounding) to 40.9% (without it).
- **Display:** Proposed, following the calculator under D-117: the moment, the share, and the probability to one decimal place, and the damages and the total to four (OQ-186).

## Limitations to show on every result

These statements come from the sources and appear with every LiFFT result:

1. "As with other lifting tools, it should be noted that specific risk estimates may not be protective for all workers" (section 5 (Discussion), converted PDF p. 9).

3. "Estimates of percentage of gender representation in the labor force suggest that males comprised approximately 75% and women 25% of jobs involving materials handling (Gabriel and Schmitz, 2001), similar to the mix in the automotive database described above (Sesek, 1999). 4. "It should be apparent that ease of use was a priority in the design of this tool, and as such, other factors associated with LBP risk (such as trunk flexion) were not included" (section 5 (Discussion), converted PDF p. 9).
5. 7. The fatigue relationship comes from cadaveric spines: "It was assumed that the same relationship holds in vivo" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 2).
