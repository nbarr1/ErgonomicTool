## What the method assesses

LiFFT estimates the cumulative low back load of a job's lifting tasks and relates the daily total to the risk of a low back outcome. The source describes it in these terms:

- **Purpose:** "the Lifting Fatigue Failure Tool (“LiFFT”), that can be used to estimate cumulative spinal loading associated with lifting tasks with three simple inputs (load weight, peak horizontal distance from spine to load, and repetition)" (section 1, converted PDF pp. 1-2).
- **Goal:** "Our goal in developing this tool was to develop a user-friendly method to estimate the risk of LBP/LBDs resulting from CD associated with variable magnitude loads and lifting repetitions" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 2).
- **Summary:** The tool "requires only the PLM (horizontal distance from spine to load times the weight of load) for a lifting task and number of repetitions performed for that task. A measure of CD is calculated based upon these values and can be summed across numerous tasks to derive a “daily dose” of CD" (section 5 (Conclusions), converted PDF pp. 9-10).
- **Model chain:** "Estimates of CD were developed by estimating the spinal compression associated with each LM, comparing these compression estimates to the compressive strength of an “average” spine (approximately 6 kN; Jager and Luttmann, 1991), and multiplying the calculated damage per cycle (DPC; derived from studies of fatigue failure of spinal motion segments) by the number of repetitions experienced during the task at hand" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 2).
- **Outcome behind the risk estimate:** The worked example reports "an estimated risk of 25.39% of experiencing a LBD" (section 2.2, converted PDF p. 4). The risk relationship comes from the LMM database, which "consists of two levels for LBD outcome. Low-risk jobs were categorized as those having no injuries and no turnover for the preceding three-year period. High-risk jobs were those having at least 12 injuries per 200,000 hours of exposure" (section 3, converted PDF p. 4). Figure 6's caption describes "the risk of LBD derived from the LMM database" (converted PDF p. 14).
- **Body region:** Back (low back). The distance is measured from "the L5-S1 vertebral segment" (section 2.2, converted PDF p. 3), and the outcomes are "LBP/LBDs" throughout. The controlled vocabulary maps this to the region "back."
- **Body side:** The source doesn't address body side, and the method takes no side input.
- **Sex and anthropometry:** The method takes no sex or anthropometry input. The compression model used "an individual of average anthropometry (blended male and female)" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 3).

### Development and validation basis

These statements describe how the authors built and tested the method. They're context for the reviewer and for the limitations, not calculation steps:

- **Fatigue data behind Eq. (1):** Cadaveric data from Brinckmann et al. (1988) and Gallagher et al. (2005, 2007) "were analyzed using a Weibull approach to estimate the probability of spine failure at varying levels of estimated ultimate strength of motion segments" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 2). For censored specimens, the authors estimated "the number of cycles to reach a given criterion level of damage (10 mm displacement)" by "linear estimation" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 2).
- **LMM database:** 235 jobs, 124 low-risk and 111 high-risk (section 3, converted PDF p. 4; section 3.1, converted PDF p. 5). "In the LMM database, Lifting Frequency is provided in lifts/hr, so this value was multiplied by eight to get an estimate of the daily dose of CD" (section 3.1, converted PDF p. 5). The conclusions describe these as "mono-task jobs (i.e., jobs with no rotation)" (section 5 (Conclusions), converted PDF p. 10).
- **Effect of the LMM design:** "The strength of relationship for the LBD outcome from this database may be due in part to the fact that low risk and high risk outcomes were separated by eliminating the medium risk category, making the relationship more pronounced" (section 5 (Discussion), converted PDF p. 8).
- **Automotive database:** 667 jobs, with 304 workers analyzed in six subsets of 179-304 subjects (section 3, converted PDF pp. 4-5). "Only jobs with well-defined lifting activities were included (administrative jobs or jobs that did not have well defined tasks were not analyzed)" (section 3.2, converted PDF p. 5). Jobs had "several different tasks (up to six), for which CD was calculated by summing the exposure calculated for each task" (section 3.2, converted PDF p. 6).
- **Automotive subjects:** "The subjects ranged in height from 147-203 cm (mean = 174.8 cm ± 9.4), weighed between 45-159 kg (mean = 84.8 kg ± 17.7), and were 20-70 years of age (mean = 41.6 years ± 10.9). The subjects were 72.7% male and 27.3% female" (section 3, converted PDF p. 5).

### Peak load moment

The source defines the peak load moment as "the weight of the lifted object multiplied by the horizontal distance of the load to the spine" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 2) and as the "horizontal distance from spine to load times the weight of load" (section 5 (Conclusions), converted PDF p. 9). In symbols (this specification's notation; the source states the product in words):

```text
PLM = load weight × maximum horizontal distance        (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 2; section 5 (Conclusions), converted PDF p. 9)
```

The worked example multiplies a mass by a length: "(40 cm × 13 kg = 51 Nm)" (section 2.2, converted PDF p. 4). The source prints neither the conversion from centimeters to meters nor the value of g that turns kilograms into newtons (OQ-183). Recomputation, not printed: 0.40 m × 13 kg × 9.81 m/s² = 51.01 N·m, and with 9.80665 m/s² the product is 50.99 N·m. Both round to 51, so the example doesn't fix g.

### Damage per cycle

### Cumulative damage for each task

The source states the step in words: "The DPC is then multiplied by the number of repetitions performed to estimate the total CD associated with that task" (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 3), and, for the automotive validation, "Repetitions for each task were available and were multiplied by the DPC to get the CD on a task basis" (section 3.2, converted PDF p. 6). In symbols (this specification's notation):

```text
task CD = DPC × repetitions per day        (section 2.1, converted PDF p. 3; section 3.2, converted PDF p. 6)
```

The worked example words the step differently: "That value is then converted to an estimate of task CD by multiplying this result by the number of repetitions performed in the day (400) and the DPC estimate of 0.000011, as derived in Section 2.1" (section 2.2, converted PDF p. 4). Read literally, "this result" is the peak load moment, which gives 51 × 400 × 0.000011 = 0.2244 (recomputation, not printed). That doesn't match the printed 0.0043, while DPC × repetitions (0.0044) matches it to within rounding. This specification follows section 2.1.

### Daily cumulative damage for the job

The source states the step in words: "The estimated CD accrued during the entire work day may be calculated by summing the CD estimates for each subtask" (section 2.2, converted PDF p. 4), and "the “daily dose” of CD associated with LBP/LBD risk may be estimated by summing the individual subtask CD estimates" (section 5 (Discussion), converted PDF p. 7). In symbols (this specification's notation):

```text
daily CD = Σ task CD, over every task in the job        (section 2.2, converted PDF p. 4; section 5 (Discussion), converted PDF p. 7)
```

The engine sums the tasks in a fixed order, as part 2 of the build prompt requires.

## Units

The manuscript works in kilograms, centimeters, newton-meters (printed "Nm"), and kilonewtons ("kN"), and it prints no English units. Its peak load moment multiplies a mass by a length, and the value of g that converts the mass to a force isn't printed (OQ-183). The engine stores canonical SI units and converts inputs with the shared conversion factors, as part 2 of the build prompt requires. Once g is decided, equivalent metric and English inputs give the same stored result, because the engine converts both to SI before it computes.

The reference calculator works in inches, pounds, and foot-pounds, with a metric option. Its units aren't a source for this method.

**Observation on Eq. (1), not printed and not used by this specification.** Suppose the damage per cycle is the reciprocal of Eq. (1)'s cycles to failure, as "the inverse" reads. Then a damage per cycle of 0.000011 needs N = 1/0.000011 = 90,909.09 cycles. With %US as a percentage number, Eq. (1) gives that when %US = ln(902,416 / 90,909.09) / 0.162 = 14.17.
